What was compared

This project simulates a "People You May Know" feature. The goal was to predict which pairs of users in a 100,000-user network are friends. Some friendships were hidden from every method first (80% training, 10% validation, 10% test), and each method then had to tell the hidden real friendships apart from an equal number of fake pairs.

A two-layer Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) that uses each user's age group, interests and country plus the friendship graph was compared against three classic methods that use only the graph. All methods were scored on the same hidden test friendships.

The baseline algorithms

Common Neighbors

Counts the friends two users share. More mutual friends means a higher score.

Jaccard Coefficient

Shared friends divided by all distinct friends of the two users. It keeps people with huge friend lists from always winning.

Adamic-Adar Index

Counts shared friends too, but a shared friend with few connections counts more than a very popular one.

These three only look at who is friends with whom, so they ignore age, interests and location. Random Walk with Restart was proposed but not tested in this project.

What the results show

The GCN is much better at telling real friendships from fake ones. Its AUC of 0.814 means a real friendship outscored a fake pair about 81% of the time, versus 55% for the three baselines. Its validation and test scores were almost identical, so it generalized instead of memorizing.

The baselines are better at the top of the recommendation list. On Precision@10 they scored 0.0072 to 0.0084, about 18 to 21 times the GCN's 0.0004.

Why the baselines score so low on AUC. They can only give a pair a non-zero score if the two users already share a friend. That was true for just 10.9% of real test friendships (and 0.1% of fake pairs), so the other 89% look the same as fake pairs to them. When they do have a mutual friend to count, though, they are precise.

The two kinds of method seem to capture different information: features say who is generally likely to be friends, and mutual friends are a sharp signal for the minority of pairs that have them. That is the motivation for the hybrid approach in the project proposal.

Limitations of the two-layer GCN

  • Limited features. The model sees age group (six buckets, not exact age), interests and country. It does not see city, and 12.7% of the friendships in this dataset are between people in the same city, so a strong signal is missing.
  • Trained on an easier question than Precision@10. Training teaches it to separate real friends from random strangers. Precision@10 asks it to pick the true friend from many similar people, which needs much finer ranking.
  • Only two hops. Two layers means each user only hears from friends and friends of friends.
  • Simple scoring. Pairs are scored with a dot product of two embeddings, which is a limited way to combine two users.
  • Cannot handle new users well. A GCN is transductive. Users with no friends are represented only by their own features, and new users would need retraining.
  • Synthetic data. Friendships were generated by a rule using age, interests and location plus randomness, so the results show how well each method recovers those patterns, not how it would do on a real social network.
  • Small evaluation. One random seed, and Precision@10 averaged over 500 users, so the numbers can shift between runs. Users with no friends have no hidden answers, so cold-start behavior was not tested.

Ideas to improve Precision@K

These are future directions. None of them has been tested in this project yet.
  • Add city and exact age to the node features, for example with a learned city embedding, since one-hot columns for about 25,000 cities would be too large.
  • Build the hybrid model by feeding Common Neighbors, Jaccard and Adamic-Adar scores into the pair-scoring step alongside the learned embeddings.
  • Train with harder negatives. Sample fake pairs that are similar in age, interests and location, so training matches what Precision@K asks.
  • Rank in two stages. Take friends-of-friends and similar users as candidates, then let the model re-rank only those instead of searching all 100,000 users.
  • Try GraphSAGE and a learned scoring layer instead of a dot product, and tune settings such as embedding size and epochs using validation scores.
  • Evaluate more carefully with more users, larger K (such as 50 or 100) and several random seeds.
